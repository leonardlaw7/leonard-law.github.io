Ask most people what worries them about using AI in financial research and you get the same word back: hallucination. The model will tell you a bond has a restricted-payments basket of USD 200mn when the offering memorandum says USD 150mn, and it will say it in the same fluent, assured tone it uses for everything else.

The usual response is to ask whether the model is accurate enough to trust. I think that is the wrong question. A model is a probabilistic system. Asking it to be trustworthy is asking it to be something it is not. So I apply the innate scepticism of a credit analyst to AI output, but design quality-control (QC) checks in the workflow to minimise omissions and make output easily verifiable by a human.

## Every claim points back to the page

When my indenture-analysis tool reads an offering memorandum, it does not just hand back a tidy summary of the covenants. Every extracted field comes with two things attached: the exact page of the OM it came from, and the verbatim quote it was drawn from. The output is an interactive verifier where each field links straight to its source. If I want to check the debt-incurrence test, I click it and I am looking at the actual sentence, in the actual document, on the actual page.

## Checking the checker

Before I ever see the output, an automated QC pass runs over it. If a quote cannot be found where it is supposed to be, that is flagged rather than quietly shipped. This catches the failure mode that matters most: the plausible-looking fabrication. A wrong answer that announces itself is a minor inconvenience. A wrong answer wearing the costume of a right one is the thing that ends up in a published note.

## A second, independent look

The last layer is triangulation. Where I have a third-party report (generated by another human or AI process) covering the same bond covenants, I run a cross-check that compares my extracted terms against it, field by field. Two summaries built from the same document by different processes should agree. Where they diverge, one of them is wrong, and it is worth finding out which.

## The reframe

None of this makes the model more accurate (accept that the accuracy of the underlying extraction is never 100%). What changes is that I never take the first-pass output at face value. Every field is sourced, every source is verified, and every extraction is reconciled against a second view. The output stops saying trust me and starts saying here is the page, check it yourself.

I find that reframing quietly liberating. "AI hallucinates" is usually deployed as a reason not to use these tools in serious work. Treated as a design constraint rather than a dealbreaker, it becomes something you engineer around. You assume the model will sometimes be wrong, and you build a system where being wrong is cheap to catch.
